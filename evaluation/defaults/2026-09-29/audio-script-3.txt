Alex: Hi Jordan, can a short time management training actually reduce students’ stress?

Jordan: That was the study’s question. Häfner, Stock, and Oberst tested whether training would increase perceived control of time and reduce perceived stress, measured with the tension scale, in undergraduate students.

Alex: Who took part, and what did the training involve?

Jordan: The main analyses used 23 undergraduate students from a medium-sized German university who completed all questionnaires. The training lasted 4 h and focused on prioritizing, goal setting, strategy development, daily planning, and monitoring task completion.

Alex: When were they measured?

Jordan: Directly before training, 2 weeks after, and 4 weeks after. Students reported time management behaviour, perceived control of time, tension, and demands, meaning perceived, self-reported demands like workload and external duties.

Alex: The design confused me. There was no control group, right?

Jordan: Right. They used a “non-equivalent dependent variable design”: they tracked variables expected to change, tension and perceived control of time, plus demands, which were not expected to change.

Alex: So what happened after the training?

Jordan: Time management behaviour increased. The increase from before training to 4 weeks after was significant, while the increase at 2 weeks was nearly significant, suggesting transfer to daily work at least to some extent.

Alex: And the main outcomes?

Jordan: Tension decreased significantly at both follow-ups: the means were 3.30 before training, 2.96 after 2 weeks, and 3.03 after 4 weeks. Perceived control of time showed only a tendency at 2 weeks, but a significant increase by 4 weeks, from 2.75 to 3.05.

Alex: Did demands change too?

Jordan: No significant changes were found for demands at either follow-up, which supported the authors’ interpretation that the training affected the intended variables. But they call it preliminary: the sample was small, about half dropped out before the second measurement, and future studies need larger samples and stronger comparison designs.
