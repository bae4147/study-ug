Alex: So this paper asks a very student-life question: can time management training actually reduce stress?

Jordan: Exactly. Häfner, Stock, and Oberst studied undergraduate students at a medium-sized German university, using a 4 hours time management training.

Alex: Small sample, right?

Jordan: Yes. Forty-eight students started, but the main analyses used the 23 who completed all questionnaires: directly before training, 2 weeks after, and 4 weeks after.

Alex: And stress here means what the paper calls the tension scale?

Jordan: Right. Perceived stress was measured with “tension,” including mental fatigue, trouble relaxing, and nervousness. They also measured perceived control of time, time management behaviour, and demands.

Alex: Demands as in perceived, self-reported demands, like workload and external duties?

Jordan: Exactly. And demands were important because the design did not use a separate control group.

Alex: Ah, so how did they try to handle that?

Jordan: They used a “non-equivalent dependent variable design.” Basically, if tension and perceived control of time change after training, but demands do not, that supports the idea that the training mattered.

Alex: So what was in the training?

Jordan: It focused on prioritizing, goal setting, strategy development, daily planning, and monitoring. Students reflected on academic and extracurricular activities, set concrete goals and deadlines, planned steps, considered obstacles, scheduled study work, and monitored task completion.

Alex: Very “make the chaos visible,” but in research language.

Jordan: Pretty much. And the students mostly had no, little, or moderate prior experience with time management strategies.

Alex: What happened after the training?

Jordan: Two weeks after training, students reported significantly less perceived stress, measured as tension, compared with before training.

Alex: Did they feel more in control of time right away?

Jordan: Not significantly at 2 weeks. The paper reports a tendency in the expected direction.

Alex: So control took longer?

Jordan: Yes. Four weeks after training, students reported significantly less perceived stress and significantly more perceived control of time than before training.

Alex: And demands?

Jordan: No significant changes in demands at either 2 weeks or 4 weeks.

Alex: That matters because their perceived workload and duties did not significantly change, but their tension did.

Jordan: Exactly. The authors interpret that as supporting the conclusion that the training led to lower perceived stress and higher perceived control of time.

Alex: Did students actually use the strategies?

Jordan: They reported a significant increase in time management behaviour by 4 weeks after training, and a nearly significant increase at 2 weeks.

Alex: Any caveats?

Jordan: Definitely. The authors call it a preliminary study, mainly because the sample was small and there was no control group or pseudo-training group.

Alex: So key takeaway: promising, not definitive.

Jordan: Yes. A short time management training might benefit undergraduate students’ well-being, but the authors say larger intervention studies are needed for more robust conclusions.
