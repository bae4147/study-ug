Alex: Okay, this paper is basically asking: can a short time management training reduce students’ perceived stress?

Jordan: Exactly. And in this paper, perceived stress means the “tension” scale: mental fatigue, trouble relaxing, and feelings of nervousness.

Alex: Good catch. Not stress in every possible sense.

Jordan: Right. They started with 48 undergraduate students at a medium-sized German university, but the main analyses used the 23 who completed all questionnaires.

Alex: Those questionnaires were before training, then 2 weeks after, then 4 weeks after?

Jordan: Yes. The training itself lasted 4 hours, in groups of about ten students, and it was run in the middle of the semester.

Alex: What did students actually learn?

Jordan: The training focused on prioritizing, setting concrete and challenging goals, developing strategies, daily planning, and monitoring task completion.

Alex: So basically: decide what matters, make a plan, schedule it, and check whether you did it.

Jordan: That’s the spirit, as the paper describes it. The authors expected perceived control of time to go up and tension to go down.

Alex: And they also measured “demands,” right?

Jordan: Yes, perceived, self-reported demands: things like amount of work and external duties. That was the control variable that should not change.

Alex: Because if everything changed, maybe it was just the semester shifting around.

Jordan: Exactly. They used what’s called a non-equivalent dependent variable design: one group, but with outcomes that should change and a related variable that should not.

Alex: So what happened?

Jordan: Two weeks after training, students reported significantly less tension than before training. Perceived control of time showed only a tendency in the expected direction.

Alex: Ah, so stress moved first, control took longer?

Jordan: That’s how the authors interpret the pattern. Four weeks after training, students reported significantly less tension and significantly more perceived control of time than before training.

Alex: And demands?

Jordan: No significant changes at 2 weeks or 4 weeks.

Alex: Nice for the design. Did they actually use the strategies?

Jordan: They reported more use of time management behaviour. The increase was nearly significant at 2 weeks and significant at 4 weeks.

Alex: Any caveats?

Jordan: Big ones. Small sample, about half dropped out before the second measurement, and there was no separate control group or pseudo-training group.

Alex: So the takeaway is cautious.

Jordan: Yes. The authors say time management training might be beneficial for undergraduate students’ well-being, but more intervention studies with larger samples are necessary.
