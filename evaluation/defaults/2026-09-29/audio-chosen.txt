Alex: Let's unpack Häfner, Stock and Oberst's time management training study.

Jordan: The question was whether time management training could raise undergraduates' perceived control of time and reduce perceived stress, measured with the "tension" scale.

Alex: Who took part, and how was it set up?

Jordan: Forty-eight local undergraduates started; the main analyses used the 23 who completed questionnaires directly before training, 2 weeks after and 4 weeks after.

Alex: What was the training?

Jordan: It lasted for 4 hours and focused on prioritizing, goal setting, strategy development, daily planning and monitoring. Students set concrete challenging proximal goals with deadlines, planned steps, considered obstacles and monitored completed tasks.

Alex: Without a separate control group, how did they check the effect?

Jordan: They used a "non-equivalent dependent variable design": track variables expected to change, perceived control of time and tension, alongside demands, which should not change.

Alex: And "demands" means?

Jordan: Perceived, self-reported demands: the amount of work and external duties students said they faced. The training aimed to help students handle demands, not change them.

Alex: What did they find after 2 weeks?

Jordan: Stress on the tension scale decreased significantly, from a mean of 3.30 to 2.96. Perceived control of time increased from 2.75 to 2.89, but only as a tendency in the expected direction.

Alex: And after 4 weeks?

Jordan: Compared with before training, stress was significantly lower, with a mean of 3.03, and perceived control of time was significantly higher, with a mean of 3.05. Demands showed no significant change at either follow-up.

Alex: Did students actually use the strategies?

Jordan: The authors report a nearly significant increase in time management behaviour after 2 weeks and a significant increase after 4 weeks, suggesting transfer to daily work at least to some extent.

Alex: What are the main cautions?

Jordan: The study was preliminary: the sample was small, about half left before the second measurement, and there was no experimental control group or pseudo-training group.

Alex: So the key takeaway?

Jordan: In this sample, training was followed by lower tension, later higher perceived control of time, and no significant change in perceived demands.
